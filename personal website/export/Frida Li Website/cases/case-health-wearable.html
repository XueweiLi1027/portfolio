Understanding Health Wearable Users: A User Archetype Study

A new product team at Anker had to ship the company's first health wearable with no prior user research. I led a mixed-methods study (10 in-depth interviews and a ~300-person validation survey) to build four user archetypes, a jobs-to-be-done (JTBD) map, and the v1 product roadmap.

01Background & Problem

In early 2025, Anker leadership identified a strategic opportunity in the health wearables category. A new cross-functional team of a PM, designers, and engineers was assembled to ship the company's first health-monitoring device. Desk research narrowed the core user to people living with chronic conditions, but the team had no existing user research, no defined target user, and no map of unmet needs to anchor v1.

I joined as the sole UX researcher to understand how this group fits monitoring devices into their daily lives, and surface where the product opportunities live.

Three research questions

  1. What archetypes describe current health-wearable users with chronic conditions?

  2. What pain points and unmet needs could become product opportunities?

  3. What core features should our first-generation product include to win share?

02Challenge

My ideal plan was a five-month, two-phase study: a large-scale survey (target n of 385 to 400) followed by a week-long contextual-inquiry field study with 6 to 8 users per segment. Two weeks in, two walls collapsed it.

What this forced
An adapted two-phase plan: qual first to define archetypes from a smaller interview sample, then a smaller validation survey on a re-grown beta panel, directional rather than statistically definitive.

03Approach

I inverted the original order to run qualitative first, then quantitative, so the small interview sample could define what we'd validate, rather than guessing categories before the survey.

Phase 01 · Qualitative

  • 10 in-depth interviews across diverse demographics and chronic conditions.
  • Designed the interview guide, moderated every session, coded all responses.
  • Coded each transcript on a 1 to 5 scale across health condition severity, mindset, technology fluency, and data-interaction frequency.
  • Aggregated those dimensions into two factors, Health Complexity and Engagement, and plotted interviews onto a 2×2 matrix.
  • Synthesized four user archetypes and a Jobs-to-be-Done map for each.

Phase 02 · Quantitative

  • Worked with user-ops to grow the beta panel from 20k → 70k during Phase 1.
  • Fielded a validation survey to test which Phase 1 pain points and unmet needs held up at scale.
  • Effective sample n≈300: less precision than the original 385 to 400 target, but enough directional confidence to prioritize features.
Limitation flagged upfront
Archetypes built from a 10-interview sample do not statistically represent the entire target population. I documented this in writing so stakeholders could calibrate the readout, and so the case for next-year's research investment had its argument already built.

04Findings

Ten interviews plotted onto the 2×2 matrix produced four distinct archetypes. Each had its own relationship to health data, device, and product opportunity.

For the Data-Driven Co-Manager, the job is to monitor health data continuously, day and night. Today they rely on an Apple Watch, but its battery life is shorter than the monitoring window they need, leaving gaps overnight and in the morning. The workaround is telling:

Workaround surfaced
Participants owned two Apple Watches and alternated them, so one was always charged. The need is "no gaps in monitoring". Battery longevity is the v1 product opportunity hiding behind a workaround.

From insight to roadmap

  • Worked with PMs, designers, and engineers to narrow from four archetypes to two target users, anchored on hardware constraints and market viability.
  • Co-defined a v1 product roadmap built around the validated pain points.
  • Final deliverable: user insights, JTBD map, and prioritized product recommendations.

06What I Did

I joined the new product team as its only researcher and owned the study end to end, from framing the questions through to the v1 roadmap handoff.

  • Set the research strategy. Framed the three research questions and inverted the plan to qualitative first, so the interview sample could define what the survey would later validate.
  • Ran the qualitative phase. Designed the guide, moderated all ten in-depth interviews, and coded every transcript across four behavioral dimensions.
  • Built the archetypes. Reduced those dimensions to Health Complexity and Engagement, plotted interviews on a 2×2 matrix, and synthesized four archetypes with a jobs-to-be-done map for each.
  • Validated at scale. Partnered with user-ops to grow the beta panel, then fielded the validation survey and analyzed which pain points held up.
  • Delivered the readout. Translated findings into the v1 feature priorities, presented to leadership, and documented every limitation in writing for the team to calibrate against.

07Lessons & Reflection

The biggest lesson was about naming limitations early, in writing.

When the panel collapsed two weeks in, the temptation was to quietly downscale the study and hope the smaller sample held up at readout. Instead, I documented every trade-off upfront: the archetypes were built on 10 interviews, the survey was directional, the field study was cut, and a follow-up was needed.

That same caveat, the one I worried would weaken the readout, became the PM's argument to leadership for next year's research investment. Naming the boundary built more long-term collaboration than smoothing it over ever could.

Lessons

  • Naming research limitations upfront builds long-term collaboration. The boundary I flagged at readout later became the argument for the next investment.
  • Qual-first is the right pivot when a market is unknown. Letting interviews define the categories the survey validates beats guessing closed-response scales.
