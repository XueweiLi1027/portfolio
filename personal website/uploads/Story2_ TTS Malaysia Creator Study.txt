S: During my internship at TikTok, TikTok Shop Malaysia had launched six months earlier and organic growth had plateaued. Backend data showed creators were already driving 14% of purchases with zero active investment. Leadership wanted to know if activating creators could push that number higher, and my PM needed a study to support their recommendation.
T: I needed to design a study answering whether creator influence could grow beyond 14% and where that growth would come from. I identified three user segments through backend data: creator-influenced buyers, exposed non-buyers, and unexposed non-buyers. Each segment answered a different strategic question. Group 1 told us what works. Group 2 told us what blocks conversion despite reach. Group 3 told us whether expanding reach would matter.
A: My ideal approach was mixed methods: 24 in-depth interviews across the three segments, followed by a 1,200-person survey. Power analysis confirmed 400 per segment would detect differences of 10 percentage points at 95% confidence and 80% power. But the PM needed results in three weeks for a leadership meeting, so I adapted to a survey-only approach.
To preserve qualitative depth without interviews, I embedded open-ended fields after every "why" closed question and added "other, please specify" options throughout. I borrowed closed answer categories from a TikTok Shop China study that had tackled the same creator growth problem, which accelerated design but introduced real risk. Malaysia's trust dynamics, multi-ethnic and multi-religious context, and competitive landscape with Shopee and Lazada differ significantly from China. Before the readout, I flagged both limitations in writing: the sample size risk from low response rates with no incentive budget, and the risk that China-borrowed categories might miss Malaysia-specific reasons. I made clear the study could deliver surface-level signals, not definitive conclusions, and that a deeper follow-up would be needed.
The survey ran 8 to 10 minutes, distributed in-app to over 12,000 backend-matched users in English and Malay. After data cleaning, which filtered by age, time-on-survey, IP address, and cross-referenced behavioral answers against backend data, we landed on roughly 2,500 effective responses, about 830 per segment.
R: The key finding was that creator influence is a commercial mechanism. Users buy through creators because of price, promotions, and product clarity, not because they're fans. That reframed how leadership thought about the creator program entirely. They signed off on a creator partnership program built around commercial tools rather than brand relationships, and they approved the deeper follow-up research I had flagged. The study also became the first user segmentation playbook for creator commerce on TikTok Shop Malaysia, and later studies reused it for adjacent questions like return reasons and re-engagement.
Lessons Learned:
1/ A survey-only approach can still surface the "why" if you place open-ended fields and "other, please specify" options deliberately at every "why" closed question. The trade-off is response rate, suitable when participant incentive is not a concern.
2/ Borrowing prior research from another market accelerates closed answer design but risks miscoding reasons unique to the new context. Pairing borrowed scales with open-ended fields is what surfaces the unknowns. In this case, that surfaced Shopee and Lazada by name, neither of which appeared in any borrowed answer set.


Full Writeup版
Context and Business Goal
This project took place in 2023 during my full time internship at TikTok, on a product called TikTok Shop. It’s an e-commerce platform that had achieved major success in China and was expanding into the Southeast Asia market.
I worked on TikTok Shop Malaysia, which had launched 6 months prior through a Shop tab in the TikTok app’s navigation bar. New customer growth happened organically and then plateaued. Our backend data showed creator content was already driving 14% of purchases with zero active investment. The remaining purchases came from users browsing the Shop tab directly, in app search, and brand promotions. Leadership wanted to know if activating creators could grow that number, and my PM came to me to build the study that would inform their recommendation.
Research Goal and Questions
Given the context, the primary research question for me was: can creator influence on TikTok Shop purchases grow beyond 14%, and where would that growth come from?
I broke this question into three sub questions, each tied to a specific user segment:
* For users who already buy through creators, what role does the creator actually play?
* For users who see creator content but do not buy, what blocks them?
* For users not yet exposed, is there latent demand we could unlock?
Best Practice
To answer these questions, the best practice approach was a mixed methods study, qualitative first followed by quantitative.
A mixed-methods approach was necessary because the research questions sat at two levels: understanding the why behind user behavior required depth and probing that surveys cannot capture, but the PM also needed defensible numbers to take to leadership, which interviews alone cannot provide. Qualitative had to go first because Malaysia was a new market and I did not know which China findings would transfer, so running the survey first would have meant guessing at the closed response categories and risking sizing the wrong pain points. Quantitative had to go second because interviews could surface vivid themes but not tell us whether 70% or 7% of users felt that way, and the PM needed prevalence data to defend the recommendation at the leadership level.
I designed three user segments, identified through backend data: 1/ Group 1: Creator influenced buyers, who clicked from creator content and completed a purchase; 2/ Group 2: Exposed non buyers, who viewed Shop content but did not purchase; 3/ Group 3: Unexposed non buyers, who had zero Shop exposure and no purchase history. Each segment answered a different strategic question. Group 1 told us what works. Group 2 told us what blocks conversion despite reach. Group 3 told us whether expanding reach would matter.
Phase 1 would be 24* semi structured in depth interviews, 8 per segment, to understand the why behind user shopping behavior. This included showing participants real examples of the two creator content formats on the platform, short videos and livestreams. I would sample examples across high, medium, and low viewer to buyer conversion rates using backend data, so I could compare user reactions across performance tiers.
Phase 2 would be a follow up survey of about 1200* users, 400 per segment, to size what we discovered at scale, identifying the pain points that resonate with most users and the outliers.
The survey sample size was driven by the need to detect meaningful differences between segments at 95% confidence and 80% power, with margins of error around ±5% per segment. Power analysis confirmed 400 per segment would reliably detect differences of 10 percentage points or larger. 
An end-to-end study of this scope typically takes 6 weeks: 1 week for planning and recruitment, 2 to 3 weeks for interviews and synthesis, 1 week for survey design informed by qualitative findings, 1 week for fielding, and 1 week for analysis and reporting.

*1200 users[b][c][d]
Constraints
However, this study faced a time limit. The PM needed presentable insights in 3 weeks for a goal alignment meeting with their leadership. After talking through the trade offs with stakeholders, I went with a survey only approach. 
In this new approach, to capture the why, I added open ended fields throughout the survey, plus "other, please specify" options on key closed questions like reasons for buying or not buying. I used findings from a similar China study to design the closed answer choices. That study had been run when TikTok Shop China faced its own user growth stall and surveyed users about content creator influence, the same problem we were facing in Malaysia, which made it a strong reference point for this study.
Before moving on with the new plan, I flagged two key limitations to stakeholders so they could calibrate what this study could and could not support when reporting to leadership. The first was a sample size challenge. Open-ended questions usually drag response rates because they take more effort to answer, so I needed to field a much larger audience than usual to land on the effective sample we wanted. On top of that, I learned the team had no budget for participant incentives, which pushed the required field size even higher. Without incentive, Malaysia's usual response rate is 2 to 3%, but for this particular survey, given there were multiple open-ended questions, I estimated the rate to be 1 to 1.5%.
The second was the risk of borrowing China findings. Malaysia differs from China in ways that directly affect creator commerce. Key factors include trust dynamics, a multi-ethnic and multi-religious context, and a different e-commerce competitive landscape with Shopee and Lazada as entrenched incumbents. China findings were a useful starting point, but the closed answer choices designed from them might miss reasons unique to Malaysian users. The open ended fields partially mitigated this, but it was a real trade off worth flagging early. 
I made it clear to stakeholders that this study, run under these constraints, could only deliver surface level signals. If we saw any indication that Malaysian users were buying because of creators, the next step would have to be a deeper investment in researching the creator economy in this market.
Compromised Plan
So in this final plan, the survey was 8 to 10 minutes, distributed in app to over 12,000 backend matched users. The effective sample size came in at roughly 2,500 after cleaning, around 830 per segment. That worked out to 2%, slightly better than my 1 to 1.5% estimate, which gave us room for sub-group analysis and richer qualitative data for thematic analysis.
The survey structure had five sections: 1/screening and demographics with backend cross reference; 2/TikTok and shopping context; 3/Shop awareness and perception asked of all groups; 4/group specific deep dive with different routing for each segment, and 5/a final open ended reflection. Open-ended fields were placed after each section, plus the "other, please specify" boxes on the “why” closed questions. The survey has two versions, one in English, the other in Malay.
The study was done in three weeks. Week 1 was stakeholder alignment, survey design, translation to Malay, and internal pilot. Week 2 was backend pull and active fielding to 120,000 Malay users. Week 3 was survey integration, data cleaning (age, time, ip address, cross-reference participant’s behavioral answers with back-end data), quantitative analysis, open-ended data coding, report, and stakeholder readout.
Results
( data analysis omit)[e]
The cleaned dataset gave me roughly 830 responses per segment, enough to compare groups directly and run sub group analysis on the two exposed segments. I structured the analysis around the three sub questions I had committed to the PM, and folded the directional recommendation for each segment into the same flow so the chain from data to action stayed visible.
Group 1: what role does the creator actually play for users who already buy through them?
The top three reasons Group 1 buyers placed their last creator driven order were that the creator offered lower prices than other stores or platforms (25.7%), better promotions like coupons or buy more save more (24.1%), and that the creator clearly presented the product, including sizes, colors, and usage (19.3%). Trust signals like positive comments and reviews on the creator's videos and LIVEs followed at 18.8%. Personal brand and parasocial connection registered in the perception data, with about a third of buyers crediting creators with "interact and connect with their followers" (32.2%) or "build their personal brand and image" (31.7%), but only 6.2% said they bought because they were a fan or follower of that creator. Categories tracked the same logic: beauty and skincare (47.8%), apparel (38.8%), and fashion accessories (38.6%) led the purchases, all categories where price comparison and visual demonstration carry the deal.
The implication is that the creator works as a price and product clarity channel, not as a relationship that closes the sale. The recommendation is therefore to keep equipping creators with the commercial tools that already drive their orders: creator specific coupons, buy more save more promotions, product presentation that makes sizes, colors, and usage clear at a glance, and after sales accountability tied to the creator, since users credit the creator for the full transaction rather than only the recommendation.
Group 2: what blocks exposed users from converting?
Group 2 recognized the commercial role of creators almost as clearly as Group 1 did (68.5% versus 71.7%) and gave creators similar credit for personal brand (31.0%). What blocked them was product fit and platform competition. The top three reasons for not placing an order were "not interested in the products recommended by the creator" (26.6%), "other stores or platforms offer better promotions, buy more save more" (14.9%), and "creator prices are higher than other stores or platforms" (10.0%). A further 34.8% said the reason they did not buy had nothing to do with the creator at all, and the open ended responses named Shopee and Lazada as the more attractive alternative.
The implication is that the gap is commercial, not relational. Awareness and trust were not the issue. The deal was. The recommendation is to close the price and assortment gap against Shopee and Lazada before pushing more creator content at this group, and to sharpen the product match by category, since beauty, apparel, and accessories converted while other categories did not.
Group 3: is there latent demand among unexposed users?
Three out of four Group 3 users said they did not know any of the creators on the awareness list (73.7%) and over half did not follow any creator (53.3%). Their perception of creators as adding any personal value beyond selling sat well below 20% on every dimension, compared to about a third for exposed users. When asked what would actually encourage them to place an order with a creator, around 80% picked at least one lever and 20.5% picked "none of the above". Across the full sample, the strongest pulls were creator specific coupons (37.6%), better content quality from creators (34.8%), and creators being held accountable for after sales service (34.2%), well above the next tier like exclusive products and brands (14.4%) or inviting outside celebrities onto TTS (7.0%).
The implication is that latent demand exists but is shallow and conditional, and reach alone will not unlock it. The recommendation is to not lead with broader exposure for this segment. Activate it with the same commercial levers that work for Group 1, while accepting that roughly a fifth will not convert under any of them.
Bringing the three answers back to the primary question
Creator influence can grow beyond 14%, but the mechanism is commercial, not parasocial. The recommendation I took into the leadership readout was to treat creators as a commercial channel that competes with Shopee and Lazada on the deal, equipped with coupons, exclusive promotions, after sales accountability, and content that clearly presents the product. As a follow on, I recommended scoping a dedicated investigation into the creator economy in Malaysia, covering trust dynamics, ethnic and religious context, and the competitive deal landscape against Shopee and Lazada, since the China findings I borrowed got us to surface signals fast but the next round needs reasons unique to Malaysian users.
Impact
* Leadership signed off on a creator partnership program and on the deeper follow up research I had flagged, because the data showed a positive signal that creator influence could grow beyond the 14% baseline.
* The study secured my team's research collaboration with the Malaysia TTS product team for the follow up phase on creator commerce in this market.
* As the first research in this domain, the study built the user segmentation playbook for creator commerce work on TTS Malaysia, splitting on backend exposure and purchase behavior. The segmentation was reused on later studies on adjacent questions like return reasons and re engagement.
Lessons Learned
* Scoping under constraint pays off when the trade offs are documented upfront. Naming what the study could and could not support, in writing, kept the readout focused on actionable findings and made the case for the follow up phase easier because the boundaries had already been drawn.
* Borrowing prior research from another market accelerates closed answer design but risks miscoding reasons unique to the new market. Pairing borrowed scales with open ended fields was what surfaced Shopee and Lazada by name, neither of which sat in any borrowed answer set.
* A survey only approach can still surface the "why" if open ended fields and "other, please specify" boxes are placed deliberately at every "why" closed question. The trade off is response rate, which is why I oversampled the field.




[a]variety & saturation
[b]That sample size came from a power calculation for comparing proportions between segments, at 95% confidence interval and 80% statistical power, designed to detect differences of 10 percentage points or larger.
[c]Rules of thumb: ~400 gives you ±5%, ~1000 gives you ±3%, ~2500 gives you ±2%
[d]These two numbers protect you against two different errors:


Confidence (95%) protects against a false positive: claiming there is a difference when there is not. "I am 95% sure the difference I am seeing is real and not just sampling noise."


Power (80%) protects against a false negative: missing a difference that really exists. "I have an 80% chance of catching a real difference if one exists."
[e]First, run a significance test on each between group comparison that you plan to lean on in the readout. Most of your variables are categorical, so the right tools are the two proportion z test for a single answer choice across two segments, or chi square for the full distribution of a multi answer question. Means like income or shopping frequency take a t test or Mann Whitney depending on shape.
Second, apply a multiple comparisons correction when you scan many percentages at once. Comparing 20 reasons across three segments produces 60 tests, and a few will hit p<.05 purely by chance. Bonferroni is the conservative default. Benjamini Hochberg false discovery rate is the more practical choice when you want to keep more of the real signals.
Third, report a 95% confidence interval on each headline percentage and on the differences that drive the recommendation, not only a point estimate. A confidence interval communicates the precision of the estimate more honestly than a p value alone, especially for product partners who do not read stats.
So the directional recommendations in your draft would be stronger if each "32.2% versus 27.5%" type gap is backed by a significance test result and a confidence interval on the difference. Where a gap fails the test, soften the language from "differs" to "directionally similar" and lean on the gaps that hold up.
