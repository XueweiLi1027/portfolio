How does Professional Background Shape Legislative Behavior in China's National People's Congress?

My MA thesis at the University of Chicago, testing two theories of why delegates in an authoritarian legislature sponsor the bills they do. Drawing on an original dataset of 622 delegates and the 2,209 bills they sponsored in the 13th National People's Congress (2018 to 2022), it links professional background to bill subject matter, the first study to do so in a setting without electoral accountability.

01Background & Question

Authoritarian regimes face a hard problem: without competitive elections, how do they learn what the public actually wants, especially its discontent, before that discontent threatens the regime? One strand of scholarship answers that legislatures are the venue where this information gets collected. But that answer leans on electoral representation, which implies legislatures without elections should be largely useless for the task.

China's National People's Congress (NPC) complicates that story. Commonly dismissed as a "rubber stamp," it nonetheless shows a vibrant internal dynamic: tens of thousands of bills sponsored over the decades, yet only a tiny fraction promulgated. Drafting a bill is genuinely costly. It demands research trips, public conferences, and the political capital to gather at least 30 co-signatures. So why do delegates do it at all, and why do they so often sponsor bills unrelated to their own interests?

Primary research question
In the absence of electoral accountability, what determines the subject matter of a delegate's bill, and what drives legislators to sponsor bills at all in the face of a very low acceptance rate?

Answering this matters beyond China. Single-party regimes are among the most resilient of dictatorships, and information provision is empirically linked to regime longevity. Probing how that information surfaces without electoral contests fills a real gap in the study of authoritarian institutions.

02Theory · Two Competing Accounts

Two existing theories make the clearest predictions about what kind of information delegates provide, and why. I test them against one another.

Theory 01 · Co-optation
Expertise as the currency.
In settings where opposition parties are banned, the regime performs functional co-optation, capturing prominent professionals (doctors, lawyers, academics, military) for their specialized knowledge. These delegates signal value to regime elites by sponsoring bills inside their own domain, and should do so more as they accumulate seniority.
Theory 02 · Reputation-boost
Visibility as the currency.
Other delegates use the legislature to build a broad public reputation. Rather than staying in their lane, they branch into many policy areas to be seen as engaged, well-rounded representatives, so as they gain experience their bills should drift away from their professional background.

Two hypotheses

  1. H1

    Functional delegates sponsor increasingly professionally-congruent bills as they gain seniority. (co-optation)

  2. H2

    Non-functional delegates, especially CEOs, increasingly diversify beyond their professional domain as they gain seniority. (reputation-boost)

The hinge of both hypotheses is a single concept, professional congruence, and how it changes with a delegate's time in office.

04Method · Coding & Models

The empirical strategy is two-step: content-analytic coding to build the congruence variable, then logistic regression to test how it moves with seniority across delegate types.

Step 01 · Coding
Matching bills to backgrounds.
Each bill was read and coded for congruence against its leading sponsor's occupation. A lawyer's bill on arbitration law counts as congruent, as does a clean-energy CEO's bill on environmental protection, or a village party secretary's bill on village committees. Congruence meant local governance for cadres, a sector match for CEOs, and a field match for functional delegates.
Step 02 · Modeling
Five logistic specifications.
A binomial logit of professional congruence on terms in office. Model 1 isolates functional delegates; Models 2 to 4 the non-functional groups (combined, CEOs, cadres); Model 5 pools everything with interaction terms so the term-effect can be compared directly across delegate types.

Bill inclusion enters as a key control: because the NPC Standing Committee decides behind closed doors which bills advance, inclusion status is a readable signal of elite policy preference, and a potential confounder for why a delegate picks a given subject.

06Findings

Both hypotheses hold, and the divergence between delegate types is sharp and statistically robust, surviving controls for demographics and elite preference.

Headline finding
Seniority pulls the two groups in opposite directions. Functional delegates lean ever harder into their expertise (co-optation); CEOs and cadres branch ever wider beyond it (reputation-boost). The same legislature rewards two completely different strategies for accumulating influence.
H1 · SupportedFunctional
Expertise deepens with tenure
Each additional term raises the odds of a professionally-congruent bill by +30.6% for functional delegates.
Professionals behave exactly as the co-optation theory predicts: the longer they serve, the more they specialize, signalling their value to regime elites through domain expertise.
H2 · SupportedCEOs
Senior CEOs diversify
For CEOs the effect inverts, with each term cutting the odds of congruence by 23.1%. They are also the most prolific sponsors (5.0 bills each).
High output, low congruence, rising with experience: CEOs use the legislature to build a broad reputation rather than to push their own industry's agenda.
Mixed signalGovt & cadres
Broadest reach, best access
Cadres show the lowest congruence (26.7%) yet the highest bill-inclusion rate (57.3%): success without specialization.
Their term-effect is negative too, but their institutional position seems to win inclusion regardless of focus, hinting at political networks the data can't yet fully resolve.

Demographic patterns: who deviates, and why

GenderWomen's interests
Female cadres are the exception
Female government executives are +151% more likely to sponsor congruent bills, uniquely folding women's-interest issues into their professional domain.
Other female delegates trend the opposite way: they use sponsorship to raise women's issues over their own expertise, consistent with a well-established gendered pattern in legislative priorities.
EthnicityBeyond expertise
Minorities range wider
Ethnic-minority delegates in non-functional roles are far less congruent than their Han counterparts (Han · CEO β = +1.872).
Minority delegates are more active sponsors overall, but pursue influence beyond their professional lane, likely to speak to their communities rather than their occupation.
ControlLog-rolling?
Inclusion ↔ lower congruence
CEO bills that make the legislative plan are about 52% less likely to be congruent (β = −0.728), a possible trace of vote-trading.
The pattern hints at reciprocity networks among delegates, though confirming it would need co-sponsor data the current dataset doesn't contain. That is flagged for future work.
