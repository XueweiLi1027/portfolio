What Drives Housing Prices in U.S. High-Poverty Neighborhoods?

A team final project for MACS 30112 (Computational Content Analysis) at the University of Chicago, asking whether housing attributes or neighborhood resources better explain prices in high-poverty census tracts across New York City, Los Angeles, and Chicago — and whether those drivers hold up at the inter-city level.

01Background & Question

Housing prices are usually framed as a function of the house — square footage, age, type, condition. But in high-poverty neighborhoods the calculus is different: the neighborhood itself carries weight that a normal hedonic model can miss. Parks, public transit, hospitals, schools, crime exposure, and demographic composition all enter the price.

For high-poverty residents this matters concretely. Their housing search is constrained — by budget, by program eligibility, by where they already work and have family — and the wrong weighting in a policy or appraisal model can entrench segregation. Understanding which attributes actually drive price in these tracts is a prerequisite for evidence-based intervention.

Primary research question
What are the key drivers of housing prices in U.S. high-poverty neighborhoods, and do those drivers hold across cities?

Two-level analysis · structured as two sub-questions

  1. L1

    City-level. Within a single city, which housing and neighborhood attributes most strongly predict listing prices in high-poverty tracts?

  2. L2

    Inter-city level. Do those drivers replicate across NYC, LA, and Chicago — and where do the three cities diverge?

We chose New York City, the City of Los Angeles, and Chicago as the three representative cities — different in form (dense vertical, sprawled horizontal, gridded mid-rise), different in transit and housing stock, but each with well-documented and well-mapped high-poverty geography.

02Cities & Scope

"High-poverty neighborhood" is the U.S. Census Bureau threshold — a tract where the poverty rate is 20% or higher. We pulled the most recent neighborhood-level poverty data from three government-backed sources (NYU Furman Center, ArcGIS, Illinois Policy), filtered to that threshold, and joined it to Redfin listings inside those neighborhoods. The point cloud below plots that filter directly — red dots are the in-scope high-poverty neighborhoods (Hyde Park, where UChicago sits, is highlighted in yellow).

03Approach

We built a per-listing dataset that joined housing attributes (size, age, type, condition, asking price) with neighborhood attributes (distance to parks, transit, hospitals; crime exposure; demographics) for every Redfin listing in every high-poverty neighborhood across the three cities. Then we modeled price against both attribute families, first within each city and then across all three.

Pipeline · three stages

Stage 01 · Collection
Scrape, geocode, join.
Selenium + BeautifulSoup on Redfin for real-time asking prices and housing attributes; each city's open-data portal for spatial layers (parks, transit stops, hospitals, crime incidents); City-Data and the three poverty sources for demographic context. Distances to amenities computed per listing.
Stage 02 · Cleaning
Filter to in-scope tracts.
Listings filtered to tracts with poverty rate ≥ 20%. Outliers in price and size capped. Categorical housing-type variables collapsed into single-family, multi-family, condo / coop, other. Three cleaned CSVs ready for regression — one per city, plus an inter-city neighborhood-level table.
Stage 03 · Modeling
Two-level regression in R.
OLS regression of listing price on housing + neighborhood attributes, fit separately within each city and pooled at the inter-city level. Reported standardized coefficients to allow comparison of the relative weight of housing vs. neighborhood drivers within and across cities.
Stage 04 · Visualization
Map first, table second.
Choropleth + scatter maps via plotly + shapely + geopandas: poverty by neighborhood, listing density inside in-scope tracts, and amenity overlays. Coefficient tables and forest-style plots from ggplot2 + jtools for the regression readouts.

04Findings · Inter-city

Pooled across the three cities, the headline result was that where a house sits matters more than what the house is.

Headline finding
In U.S. high-poverty neighborhoods, neighborhood attributes outweighed housing attributes as drivers of asking price. Inside the home, the usual signals still held — but the neighborhood layer carried more weight overall.
Held · As expected Inside the house
Size, age, buyer demographics
Larger houses command higher prices. Younger houses are more popular than older. Asian buyers tend to find lower-priced listings.
The standard hedonic signals still apply in high-poverty tracts — they just don't dominate the equation the way a non-poverty-filtered model would suggest.
Unexpected Parks
Proximity ≠ premium
Being closer to a park did not mean a higher price — in high-poverty tracts it tracked with higher exposure to risky events (e.g. robbery).
The "park premium" assumed in most urban hedonic literature inverts here. In these tracts, the park is a risk variable as much as an amenity — and the listing price reflects it.
Unexpected Public transit
No preference for buses
Proximity to public transit — buses in particular — showed no premium in any of the three cities.
A reminder that "transit access" is not a single thing. In these tracts, bus access didn't act as an amenity buyers paid for — likely because reliance, not preference, drives transit use here.

05Findings · Per city

The inter-city findings held at the city level too — but each city carried its own local preference on top.

Chicago Housing type
Single-family premium
Chicago valued single-family residential properties considerably more than NYC or LA — the strongest housing-type effect across the three cities.
Consistent with Chicago's housing-stock composition: single-family is a more meaningful share of the inventory than in the other two cities, and the market prices it accordingly.
Los Angeles Business atmosphere
Commercial proximity premium
LA placed more weight on business atmosphere — proximity to commercial activity — than either Chicago or NYC.
Plausibly tied to LA's polycentric form: commercial nodes are more dispersed, so being near one matters more for daily life and re-sale.
New York City Hospital proximity
Less weight on hospitals
NYC placed less emphasis on convenience of getting to a hospital than Chicago or LA — the smallest hospital-proximity coefficient of the three.
A dense transit-rich city flattens hospital-distance effects: when most listings sit inside a short transit ride of multiple hospitals, the variable simply has less variance to price.

07Reflection

Two takeaways I carry forward from this study, methodologically and substantively.

01 · Filtering the sample is half the finding

Most urban hedonic work pools all neighborhoods together and ends up averaging away exactly the dynamics we wanted to see. Restricting to high-poverty tracts was the move that surfaced the park inversion and the transit non-effect — both of which would have been buried inside a city-wide regression. The poverty filter wasn't a constraint, it was the lens.

02 · Robustness in social science comes from the inter-city test

Each city-level finding became more credible once it survived the inter-city pool — and where the inter-city result held but a single city diverged, that divergence was a finding in its own right (Chicago's single-family premium, LA's commercial atmosphere, NYC's hospital flattening). Building the analysis as two levels rather than one turned what would have been three parallel papers into a single nested argument.

03 · Map first, table second

Before any regression we drew the hero scatter and three choropleths. They forced us to sanity-check the poverty filter, the tract joins, and the listing density before we asked R for a coefficient. Half the data-cleaning bugs we caught were caught visually — and the same maps did most of the explanatory work in the final readout.
