Can TikTok Creators Sell?

Six months after launch in Malaysia, TikTok Shop's growth had plateaued — yet backend data showed creators were already driving 14% of purchases with zero active investment. I designed and fielded a 3-week, three-segment survey (n≈2,500) that reframed creators as a commercial channel and seeded the first creator-partnership program.

01Background & Problem

In 2023, TikTok Shop launched in Malaysia through a dedicated Shop tab inside the TikTok app. New-customer growth was organic and strong at first, then plateaued six months in. Backend data showed creator content was already driving 14% of purchases with zero active investment — the remainder coming from users browsing the Shop tab, in-app search, and brand promotions.

Leadership wanted to know whether actively partnering with creators could push that 14% higher. My PM came to me to build the study that would inform their recommendation, three weeks before the leadership goal-alignment meeting.

Primary research question
Can creator influence on TikTok Shop purchases grow beyond 14%, and where would that growth come from?

Three sub-questions · one per backend-defined segment

  1. G1

    Creator-influenced buyers. What role does the creator actually play?

  2. G2

    Exposed non-buyers. What blocks conversion despite reach?

  3. G3

    Unexposed non-buyers. Is there latent demand we could unlock?

02Challenge

The best-practice approach for this brief was a six-week mixed-methods study: 24 semi-structured in-depth interviews (8 per segment), then a 1,200-person survey (400 per segment) sized via power analysis to detect 10-percentage-point differences at 95% confidence and 80% power.

Two real constraints made that plan impossible.

Constraint 01 · Timeline
Three weeks, not six.
The PM needed presentable insights in three weeks for a leadership goal-alignment meeting. Phase-1 interviews alone would have consumed the entire window.
Constraint 02 · No incentives
No budget for participant incentives.
Malaysia's typical unincentivized response rate is 2–3%. With multiple open-ended questions, I estimated 1–1.5% — pushing the field size required well above normal.
What this forced
A survey-only study that still preserved the "why" — and a written agreement with stakeholders that the output would be surface-level signals, not definitive conclusions, with a deeper follow-up to be scoped if the signal was positive.

03Approach

I designed a single survey across three backend-defined segments — creator-influenced buyers, exposed non-buyers, unexposed non-buyers — each answering a different strategic question. To preserve qualitative depth I embedded open-ended fields after every "why" closed question and added "other, please specify" options throughout. To accelerate design, I borrowed closed-answer categories from a prior TikTok Shop China study that had tackled the same creator-growth problem.

Trade-offs flagged in writing

  • Borrowed categories may miss Malaysia-specific reasons. Trust dynamics, multi-ethnic and multi-religious context, and the Shopee + Lazada-shaped competitive set don't transfer from China cleanly. Open-ends were the safety net.
  • Open-ends drag response rate. I oversampled the field to 120k backend-matched users to compensate.
  • This study delivers surface signals, not conclusions. A deeper follow-up would be needed if the signal was positive.

Analysis

  • Two-proportion z-tests across segments for headline comparisons; 95% confidence intervals on each driving proportion.
  • Thematic coding of every "why" response, cross-referenced against the closed-question patterns.
  • Structured findings around the three sub-questions; folded a directional recommendation into each.

04Findings

The headline answer to the primary question: creator influence can grow beyond 14% — but the mechanism is commercial, not parasocial. Users buy through creators because of price, promotions, and product clarity, not because they're fans.

What the open-ends caught
Shopee and Lazada showed up by name in open-ended responses — both absent from any China-borrowed answer set. That single finding reframed the real competition set for creator commerce in Malaysia.

Bringing it back to the primary question

The recommendation I took into the leadership readout was to treat creators as a commercial channel that competes with Shopee and Lazada on the deal — equipped with creator-specific coupons, exclusive promotions, after-sales accountability tied to the creator, and content that clearly presents sizes, colors, and usage. As a follow-on, I scoped a dedicated investigation into the Malaysia creator economy to cover the local reasons the borrowed scales couldn't.

05Impact

The study delivered a positive signal that creator influence could grow beyond the 14% baseline — and a written argument for the deeper follow-up needed to act on it.

06Lessons & Reflection

The two methods lessons I carry forward from this study:

01 · A survey-only study can still surface the "why"

— if open-ended fields and "other, please specify" boxes are placed deliberately at every "why" closed question. The trade-off is response rate, which is why I oversampled the field. Suitable when participant incentive isn't a lever you can pull.

02 · Borrowed scales accelerate design — but pair them with open-ends

Borrowing closed-answer categories from another market is fast, but risks miscoding reasons unique to the new context. Pairing borrowed scales with open-ended fields is what surfaced Shopee and Lazada by name, neither of which sat in any borrowed answer set. The open-ends weren't a polite addition — they were the safety net that caught the real finding.

03 · Scope under constraint pays off when trade-offs are written down

Naming what the study could and could not support, in writing, kept the readout focused on actionable findings and made the case for the follow-up phase easier — the boundaries had already been drawn.
